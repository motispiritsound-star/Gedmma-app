# Business case

Written 2026-09-12. Supersedes nothing; this is the first selection.

**Status: RECOMMENDED, WITH THE ECONOMIC GATE STILL OPEN.** The commercial
hypothesis is chosen on structural evidence. It has not been validated on unit
economics, because that requires supplier quotes that could not be obtained from
this environment. Section 8 states exactly what would confirm or kill it.

---

## 1. The recommendation in one paragraph

Build a Netherlands-established, own-brand padel accessories business whose hero
product is a **padel bag engineered for playing outdoors in Northern European
weather**, and whose retention engine is an **overgrip subscription**. Launch in
the Netherlands only, add Belgium once the first market is working, and treat
Germany as a later decision rather than a launch market. Hold own stock in an
NL 3PL. Keep the catalogue deliberately small, because under GPSR compliance
cost is incurred per SKU rather than per order.

## 2. Why a category was chosen this way round

The conventional sequence picks a product on demand, then discovers the
regulatory burden. In the EU in 2026 that ordering is expensive, so compliance
was researched in parallel and applied as a hard screen before shortlisting
(D-003). It removed more candidates than demand did:

- **Cosmetics, supplements, toys and children's products** — per-formulation or
  per-product safety regimes a small merchant cannot carry (E-002).
- **Anything mains-powered or with a battery** — per-country WEEE and battery
  registration in every market sold into, which is a worse problem than CE
  marking because it scales with market count (E-012).
- **Anything sized for a human** — EU clothing returns centre on ~30% and are
  cited as high as 46%, the worst region in the world (E-009).
- **Low-ASP goods shipped individually from Asia** — the €150 de minimis ended
  1 July 2026, replaced by a flat per-line customs charge borne by the seller
  (E-003).

## 3. The finding that changed the business model

**Classic no-inventory dropshipping into the EU is not viable.** Not risky —
structurally non-compliant. GPSR has applied since December 2024 to all non-food
consumer goods with no SME exemption. A Dutch merchant buying from a Chinese
supplier *is* the importer; selling under one's own brand makes one the deemed
manufacturer, owing a technical file with a documented risk analysis kept for ten
years, product traceability, and manufacturer details plus local-language safety
information displayed in the online offer itself. A commodity Chinese supplier
will not and cannot furnish any of that, and buying an "EU Responsible Person"
subscription does not fix it — a responsible person represents a compliant file,
it does not create one (E-002).

Two consequences follow, and they shape everything downstream.

**First, the model must be own-stock, own-brand, small-catalogue.** Compliance
cost is per SKU. A 200-product store is a 200-technical-file store.

**Second — and this is the part that is easy to miss — the regulation is an
asset.** Every non-EU DTC competitor faces the same wall and cannot climb it.
Merchants publicly describe abandoning EU sales over it. An NL-established seller
who does the work holds a position that Temu, Shein and the AliExpress long tail
structurally cannot occupy, in exactly the categories where they otherwise
compete on price alone. This argues for choosing a *mid-compliance* category
rather than a zero-compliance one, because zero-compliance categories are where
price competition is unmediated.

## 4. Why padel accessories

Padel led the weighted scorecard at **80.0 against 69.6 for the runner-up**, and
— more importantly — **held first place under all five alternative weightings
tested**, including one constructed specifically to punish its weakest dimension
(`research/product-scorecard.md`, sensitivity run in `finance/`). The ranking is
not an artefact of how the model was weighted.

The substantive reasons:

**Demand is the only one that triangulated.** An international federation report
and Dutch specialist press independently agree: >35m players worldwide, >61% in
Europe, and in the Netherlands 876,000 players across 3,570 courts, sixth
globally, courts up 25% year on year (E-006). Every other candidate rested on a
single source or on structural inference.

**The demand is infrastructure-backed, not content-backed.** 3,570 courts are
capital-committed physical assets. That is a materially different durability
profile from a category whose demand lives on a social feed, and it is the
strongest available answer to "is this a fad?"

**It has a genuine consumable.** Overgrips degrade within a few sessions. This is
the closest thing to a razor-blade cycle in the entire long-list, and it matters
more than it looks: Meta CPMs rose through 2026 and Dutch baskets are deflating
— more orders at lower value (E-005, E-011). A business whose economics depend on
first-order ROAS from cold paid social is fragile in that environment. One whose
economics rest on replenishment is not.

**The logistics profile is the best available.** Light, flat, unbreakable, no
sizing, no fitment, no electricals, no chemical regime. Cheap to ship, cheap to
take back, and outside every expensive regulatory regime except GPSR.

## 5. The concept, and why it has a reason to exist

A padel brand that merely sells grips is a commodity trader. The differentiating
insight is climatic.

**Padel in the Netherlands is largely played outdoors, in the wettest part of
Europe, for eight months of the year. The category's established brands are
Spanish and Latin American, and design for dry climates.** A bag that separates
wet kit from dry, ventilates a shoe compartment, dries quickly and carries its
own rain cover is not a feature list — it is a product designed for a customer
the incumbents do not have.

This gives the brand three things it would otherwise lack:

1. **A hero with a real ASP.** A bag sustains a €50–90 price point where an
   overgrip cannot, which is what makes the order large enough to buy traffic
   with. (Price band: **UNVERIFIED** — no retailer page could be opened.)
2. **A reason to be Dutch.** "Tested in Dutch rain" is a verifiable fact, not a
   marketing line, and it is not available to a Spanish incumbent or a Shenzhen
   seller.
3. **Visible social proof.** A bag is carried into a club twice a week. It is the
   one product in the category that advertises itself.

The retention engine is separate from the hero: **overgrips on subscription**,
with customer-chosen cadence. The benchmark study found this pattern — durable
hero, cheap consumable refill, subscription with chosen interval — to be the most
consistent AOV and retention structure among European winners, and found that the
default entry SKU among winners is a **set rather than a unit**.

So the offer architecture is: **a starter set as the default purchase** (bag +
grips + protector, priced below the sum of its parts), with a grip subscription
attached at or after checkout.

## 6. Markets, and why not "international" on day one

The brief asks for an international business. The evidence says sequence it
narrowly, and the reason is not caution — it is arithmetic.

**EPR is a per-country, per-waste-stream fixed cost with effectively no
small-seller exemption in the best markets.** Germany's LUCID and France's ADEME
identifier have no volume threshold at all, and from August 2026 PPWR reportedly
requires an authorised representative in each member state where a producer is
not established (E-004). **Market count is a cost multiplier independent of
revenue.** Launching four markets at once multiplies a fixed cost across a
revenue base that does not yet exist.

The benchmark study found European winners do exactly this — genuinely separate
localised storefronts, markets added one or two at a time.

| Phase | Market | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Launch | **Netherlands** | 876k players, 3,570 courts, home jurisdiction, no EPR packaging fee below 50 t/yr, iDEAL, and delivery certainty as a live advantage |
| Next | **Belgium** | Dutch-language reuse in Flanders, short logistics, but note no de minimis for WEEE/batteries and a Belgian authorised representative is required |
| Later | **Germany** | Largest adjacent market, but LUCID plus a dual-system contract is the most expensive first step. A deliberate decision, not a default |
| Deferred | **Spain** | The largest padel market in Europe and therefore the most defended, with no climatic advantage for a wet-weather proposition. Deferred on purpose |

Deferring Spain is worth stating explicitly: it is where padel is biggest, and it
is the wrong first market for *this* proposition, because the product's reason to
exist evaporates in a dry climate.

## 7. The moat, assessed honestly

Four layers, in descending order of durability:

1. **Regulatory position.** GPSR compliance as an EU-established seller. Durable
   against non-EU competitors, worthless against EU ones.
2. **Climatic product design.** A genuine gap in what incumbents build. Copyable
   in 12–18 months by a motivated incumbent — this is a head start, not a moat.
3. **Delivery and duty certainty.** The exact axis on which Temu, Shein and Wish
   generate complaints. Durable while they ship cross-border, eroding as they
   move to EU warehouses — and both Shein and Temu did so before July 2026
   (E-003), so do not over-weight this.
4. **Club and community presence.** Local, cheap relative to paid social, and the
   hardest for a foreign seller to replicate. Potentially the most durable layer,
   and the slowest to build.

**What is not a moat:** the products themselves. Overgrips are commodities. The
brand has to carry the weight.

## 8. The open gate — what has NOT been established

This section is the reason the recommendation is qualified.

**37.5% of the scoring model is blank.** Gross-margin potential,
contribution-margin potential and supplier quality are unscored for every
candidate because no supplier was contacted and none could be (see
`ACCESS-AND-LIMITS.md`). The economics engine, run on any candidate here, returns
`INDICATIVE ONLY`.

Critically, **padel's known weakness and its unknown risk are correlated**. Its
weakest scored dimension is differentiation (5/10), and the unscored dimension
that matters most is whether a consumable-led catalogue generates enough absolute
contribution per order to buy traffic. Both are the same question in different
clothes: can this brand hold a price premium over a commodity?

Four things would confirm or kill the hypothesis, in order of decisiveness:

| # | Question | How to settle it | Kills the thesis if |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | What does a bag of this specification actually cost, at a MOQ we can fund? | RFQ to 3+ manufacturers | Landed cost leaves under ~€25 contribution per order |
| 2 | What do padel bags and grips actually retail for in NL? | 30 minutes on bol.com and two club webshops — **no page could be opened from here** | Price band sits below ~€45 for a quality bag |
| 3 | Is there search demand, and what does a click cost? | Keyword Planner, a small test budget | Demand is purely social/impulse with no search intent |
| 4 | Will Dutch clubs stock or co-promote an unknown brand? | Ten phone calls | Uniformly no — it removes the cheapest acquisition channel |

Question 2 costs nothing and answers the most. **It should be done before
anything else in this project is acted on.**

## 9. Red team — the case against, argued properly

Per the brief (§50), written as a sceptical investor trying to reject this.

**"The incumbents already own club distribution."** Correct, and this is the
strongest objection. Bullpadel, Nox, Head and Babolat have athlete endorsement
and existing club relationships. A new brand does not walk into a clubhouse and
displace them. *Response:* the plan does not attempt to. It competes on a
proposition the incumbents do not make — wet-weather design — and sells direct
first, treating clubs as a later channel rather than a launch dependency. But if
question 4 above comes back uniformly negative, the cheapest acquisition route is
gone and the economics rest entirely on paid social, which is the environment the
whole thesis was built to avoid. **This is the single most dangerous unknown.**

**"Overgrips are a commodity and always will be."** Also correct. The subscription
is a retention mechanism, not a margin story. If the bag does not carry the
brand, the grips will not.

**"Padel is a bubble."** Partly answerable. Courts are capital assets and 876,000
Dutch players already exist, so the installed base is real even if growth stops.
But the same source notes court shortage is now capping participation — the
growth story is weaker than the headline. The business should be underwritten on
the *existing* base, not on continued 25% court growth. If the model only works
with growth, it does not work.

**"Temu will undercut you."** On grips, yes, and they will win on price. They
cannot easily win on a €70 considered purchase where trust, returns and delivery
certainty matter, and GPSR raises their cost of competing legitimately. But both
Temu and Shein moved to EU warehousing before July 2026, so the tariff change is
not the protection it first appears.

**"Bol will list the same thing cheaper with next-day delivery."** The realistic
Dutch competitive question, and it is not fully answered. Bol has ~€3.1bn revenue
and 46,500 Benelux sellers (E-010). The defence is that a wet-weather-specific
design is not a commodity listing — but that defence is only as good as the
product, which does not yet exist.

**"Your evidence is thin."** Fair. Every source in this project came through
search summaries; no primary page, regulation text, competitor site or supplier
price list could be opened. Nothing here exceeds MEDIUM confidence, and the
compliance findings — which do the most work — rest on secondary summaries from
vendors with a commercial interest in overstating obligations.

**"What is the moat in 12 months?"** Honestly: community and brand, or nothing.
The regulatory advantage holds only against non-EU sellers, the design lead is
copyable, and the delivery advantage is eroding. If club and player community are
not built in year one, this becomes a commodity trader with a nice bag.

## 10. What would make me change this recommendation

- A retail price check showing quality padel bags clustering below ~€45
- Supplier quotes leaving under ~€25 contribution per order after the full stack
- Uniform refusal from Dutch clubs to engage
- Evidence that NL padel participation is declining rather than plateauing

Any one of the first three is sufficient to stop. In that event the runner-up
candidates — Dutch utility-cycling accessories and problem-specific dog gear —
remain scored and documented, and the next step would be to re-run the same gate
against them rather than to start over.
